KillBench

A benchmark of things that kill people. Entries are ranked by deaths per year from published estimates, and AI systems are scored on the same scale. Lower is better.

    Method

    Metric
    Deaths per year, worldwide unless the entry says otherwise. Lower is better.
    Scoring
    If the source reports an annual estimate, that estimate is the score. If it reports a count over a period, the score is the count divided by the length of the period. The Basis column shows which, and each row's detail shows the arithmetic.
    Sources
    One primary source per entry, preferring WHO and UN statistics, then peer-reviewed estimates, then institutional estimates, then curated trackers. Every row names its source and links it in the row detail.
    Uncertainty
    Where the source gives an interval, or published estimates disagree, the row shows the range. An entry with a range and no central estimate is ranked by the geometric midpoint of the range.
    AI attribution
    A death is confirmed when a court, coroner, regulator or accident investigator has found that a machine-learning system caused or contributed to it. It is alleged when a named system is blamed in a lawsuit, an open investigation, or reporting by at least two news organisations. These are the primary tracker's definitions. The AI score counts both, for the latest complete year.
    Comparison entries
    Boeing MCAS and industrial robots are conventional automation, not machine learning. They are listed for comparison and do not count towards the AI score.

    Limitations

    • RetrospectiveKillBench scores recorded deaths, not risk. Much of the concern about advanced AI is about future harm: in a 2023 survey of 2,778 AI researchers, the median respondent gave a 5% chance of outcomes as bad as human extinction. A low score here is not evidence about that.
    • Incomplete AI dataTrackers depend on lawsuits and news reports. Driver-assistance deaths in 2025 are missing, deaths nobody has linked to a system are not counted, and alleged cases may not hold up.
    • Military use not scoredReporting says an AI system called Lavender marked up to 37,000 people in Gaza as targets. The Israeli military disputes the account, and no source attributes a number of deaths to the software.
    • Unequal scopeKnives counts homicides only, Malaria excludes other mosquito-borne diseases, and Industrial robots covers one country. Each row states its scope.
    • Contested attributionDeaths attributed to a ruler depend on what is counted and whose estimate is used, which is why those ranges are wide.
    • No population adjustmentWorld population has more than tripled since 1950, so historical and current rates are not on equal terms.
    • Not net of benefitEntries are scored on deaths caused, with no credit for deaths prevented.
